In one paragraph. Tuklas exists: it is a functional, full-stack web application (React front end, Express server, PostgreSQL database) at the pre-pilot stage. It has been built and checked with automated software tests only. It has not yet been used by students or teachers in a research setting, has not been validated by mathematics or educational-technology experts, and its AI features have not been evaluated with a live language model. 1. Status of Tuklas (the professor's third concern)

"Clarify whether Tuklas is already an existing and functional platform or whether it is still a proposed system. If it exists, who developed it? What features are currently operational? Has it undergone testing?"

1.1 What Tuklas is today

Tuklas is an existing, functional web application. Students use it in a web browser on a phone or computer; teachers use it to manage classes and lessons. It has a database, user accounts with three roles (student, teacher, administrator), and the features listed in Section 2. It is at the pre-pilot stage: it is complete enough to be tried with a small class, but it has not yet been tried.

1.2 Who developed it

Disclosure for the group to confirm. The platform was developed by [GROUP TO COMPLETE: name(s) of the developer(s) and their role]. Most of the programming was carried out with the help of an AI coding assistant (Claude Code, by Anthropic) working under the developer's direction; the repository's change history records this. [GROUP TO CONFIRM: wording, and whether this is acceptable under the course's rules on AI assistance]. The group should be ready to explain which parts of the design (the feature choices, pedagogy, curriculum selection, mastery rules) are their own decisions.

1.3 What content it currently contains

  • Grade 7 Mathematics, Term 1, Weeks 1–6 of the DepEd MATATAG Grade 7 Mathematics budget of work supplied by the school: 6 lessons, 6 competencies, 17 skills and a bank of 180 questions (126 numeric-answer, 34 multiple-choice, 20 true/false).
  • Topics: polygons (naming, regular and irregular, convex and non-convex), angle pairs, interior and exterior angles of polygons, percentage increase and decrease, money problems with percentages (discounts, commission, sales tax, simple interest, budgets), and rates and unit rates.
  • Lesson text, examples and questions are original to the platform; every answer was computed by code and rechecked by tests.
  • This is not the integers-and-fractions scope used as an example in the feedback. Fractions are not built. An earlier integers unit (7 lessons) from the first development stage remains in the system, but it is not part of the Term 1 content and no teacher has reviewed it. Teachers can add further lessons themselves (including by uploading a Word or PDF lesson, which becomes an editable draft).

1.4 How it has been tested (and what that does and does not show)

  • Automated software tests across 46 development phases, run with an in-memory store and on a real PostgreSQL database; browser tests that act as a teacher and a student on a laptop and a phone-sized screen; security probes of a production-mode server; and a full regression run after each phase. Examples: 69 checks on the tutor's safety against hostile student messages and hostile model output, 925 checks on the tutor's topic hints, 47 on privacy functions.
  • These tests show the software behaves as designed with simulated users and scripted AI models. They do not show that students learn more, that the feedback is pedagogically sound, or that a live AI model behaves well.

3. Answers to the twelve points

First: "Establish the actual research problem through evidence."

Response. The proposal's statement that many Junior High School students struggle because teachers cannot always give immediate, individual feedback is, as written, not yet supported by evidence. The platform cannot supply that evidence; it only becomes a data source after a pilot. The group will substantiate the problem from three sources: (a) published literature on the timing of feedback and on persistent mathematical misconceptions [GROUP TO COMPLETE: references]; (b) documented assessment results from the intended school or from national and international assessments [GROUP TO COMPLETE: source, year, figures]; and (c) interviews or classroom observations with Grade 7 mathematics teachers [GROUP TO COMPLETE: how many teachers, instrument, date, or "not yet done"]. Until then the problem will be written as a concern drawn from the literature, not as an observed fact.

Second: "Narrow the mathematical competencies covered by your study."

Response. Agreed. The study will be limited to Grade 7 and to a small set of competencies. The platform currently contains Grade 7 Term 1 (Weeks 1–6), so a realistic scope would be one or two of its topic clusters: polygons and angles; percentage change and money problems; or rates. Integers and fractions, the professor's example, are not built, so choosing them would add development work. Final choice: [GROUP TO COMPLETE: topics, based on the confirmed school, class and the school's current pacing].

Third: "Clarify the actual status of Tuklas."

Response. See Section 1 (status, developer, tested and untested features) and Section 2 (the feature list with a status label for each feature).

Fourth: "Reconsider the scope of your research design."

Response. Developmental research and mixed-methods research serve different purposes, and the group accepts that its design has several phases (Section 4C). The software-development phase has largely reached a prototype; the phases that need schools, experts and participants have not started and are the main feasibility risk. The group will state which phases are within its time and resources: [GROUP TO COMPLETE: planned phases, schedule, available people]. If time is short, the group may limit the study to development, expert validation and a small pilot, and say so openly.

Fifth: "Improve the Statement of the Problem."

Response. Accepted. The group will adopt the revised questions on design requirements, development, expert evaluation, mastery before and after, the pre/post difference, experiences, and improvements (Section 4B), and will separate evaluation of Tuklas as an educational product (accuracy, appropriateness, functionality, usability, privacy safeguards) from its possible effect on students' mastery.

Sixth: "Strengthen the evaluation of the AI-generated feedback."

Response. Agreed that accuracy must be investigated, not assumed. What exists now: expected answers are computed or checked by code; the tutor's output is screened so it does not state the answer; mistake findings cite the attempts they come from and carry a confidence value; the AI tutor can be switched off, and conversations flagged by the safety screens are visible to the teacher; and an evaluation script can replay simulated student conversations so that the replies can be scored. What does not exist yet: any review by mathematics educators, a measure of how often the Mistake Analyzer is right, and a way for a teacher to approve each tutor reply before the student sees it (replies are immediate). The group will validate the feedback with a panel of qualified mathematics educators using the rubric in Section 4E, will report errors as well as successes, and will treat every AI diagnosis as a hypothesis about a misconception until it is supported by the student's own work or explanation. Panel: [GROUP TO COMPLETE: number, qualifications, how recruited].

Seventh: "Clarify how mathematics mastery will be measured."

Response. Section 4D gives the definition the software currently uses for each indicator. These are design choices that have not been validated. The group agrees that Tuklas must not be the only evaluator of its own effectiveness: the main outcome will be an independently developed or validated mathematics test, given in the same way before and after the intervention and scored outside Tuklas [GROUP TO COMPLETE: name or source of the test, who validated it]. Tuklas's internal indicators will be reported as secondary, descriptive measures, and their agreement with the independent test can be examined. The software does not currently combine the four indicators into one mastery score; the group must either report them separately or define and justify a composite.

Eighth: "Reconsider how you will establish effectiveness."

Response. Agreed. The platform's own pre/post comparison is deliberately descriptive (differences in average scores) and makes no causal claim; it does not compute a significance test. The significance test (for example a paired-samples test appropriate to the data) will be run by the group in statistical software on the exported, pseudonymised data. The group will either include a comparison group [GROUP TO COMPLETE: feasible or not, and how] or state plainly that a one-group pretest–posttest design cannot rule out other explanations (regular instruction, practice effects, prior learning) and will limit its claims accordingly.

Ninth: "Clarify how qualitative data will be collected and analyzed."

Response. The group agrees that an AI-generated diagnosis is not evidence of what a student was thinking. Qualitative evidence will come from students' own explanations (interviews, think-aloud or open-ended written solutions) and from teacher interviews on the accuracy and usefulness of the feedback [GROUP TO COMPLETE: protocol, sample, interview guide]. Responses will be coded by thematic analysis [GROUP TO COMPLETE: coding procedure, who codes, how disagreement is resolved] and integrated with the quantitative results (for example by comparing the platform's diagnoses with what students say). With consent, students' typed messages to the tutor can also be exported from the platform as additional material.

Tenth: "Address ethical considerations and data privacy."

Response. See Section 5. In short: the platform has privacy features, but permissions and consent are study tasks that have not been done, and the choice of AI engine decides whether any student text leaves the server.

Eleventh: "Strengthen the verification of your research policy references."

Response. The group will verify the exact titles, dates and provisions of the cited documents from official sources and replace outdated agenda periods with current ones [GROUP TO COMPLETE: verified citations for CHED, DepEd Order No. 39, s. 2016, and RTU]. The software cannot verify these. On the SDGs, to be checked against the official United Nations text: Target 4.1 concerns quality primary and secondary education with relevant and effective learning outcomes, which fits a study of Grade 7 mastery; Target 4.c concerns the supply of qualified teachers, so the group should either justify the link to teacher-supervised AI or drop it.

Twelfth: "Improve the academic writing and presentation of your proposal."

Response. Accepted. The group will use cautious wording until the platform is validated, include a reference list, use consistent terms, and present one final title. Examples of claims to retire:

AvoidUse instead
"Tuklas is classroom-ready.""Tuklas is a functional prototype that has been tested with software tests and has not yet been validated with students, teachers or experts."
"Tuklas can diagnose every incorrect answer.""Tuklas attempts to classify the likely type of error from a student's responses. Its findings are hypotheses. Specific common slips are recognised for most numeric Term 1 questions; other answers receive only general findings."

"The AI gives accurate feedback.""The accuracy of the AI-supported feedback is evaluated by experts using a rubric."

D. Operational definitions of the four indicators (as implemented)

The software reports Understanding, Accuracy, Application and Consistency (research metrics, version RESEARCH_V1) and, separately, a rule-based mastery level (version MASTERY_V1). The weights and thresholds are design choices that have not been validated; the group must justify them, adjust them, or replace them. Each metric reports "insufficient evidence" below a minimum number of attempts.

IndicatorSuggested meaning (professor)How Tuklas currently computes itGap
AccuracyCorrectness of computations and solutions.Correct attempts divided by valid attempts (at least 3 attempts).Counts correct final answers only, not the quality of the working.
UnderstandingAbility to explain concepts and recognise relationships.A weighted score (at least 4 attempts): 50% accuracy without hints, 20% checkpoint score, 15% variety of question formats, 15% a reduction when the Mistake Analyzer reports conceptual misunderstandings or prerequisite gaps.Inferred from answers; it does not measure explanation. Needs open-ended explanations or interviews.
ApplicationUse of concepts in contextual or unfamiliar problems.Accuracy on questions labelled "application" or "higher-order", with higher-order items weighted 1.5 (at least 2 such attempts). The mastery rules use item types (numeric, equation, ordering, matching, hard) as a proxy.Depends on the cognitive-level labels given to questions, which no teacher has verified.
ConsistencyStability of performance across assessments.From the spread of accuracy across segments of the attempt history and the longest correct streak, scaled by overall accuracy (at least 5 attempts). The mastery rules use a separate composite: run stability 35%, recent-versus-overall agreement 25%, independence from hints 20%, easy-versus-medium gap 20%.Two slightly different definitions exist; the group should choose one.

Levels. Research metrics: low (below 0.60), developing (0.60–0.74), proficient (0.75–0.89), high (0.90 and above). Mastery levels, with the thresholds kept in one configuration file: learning (fewer than 5 attempts, or overall accuracy below 60%, or recent accuracy below 50%); developing (at least 5 attempts and what remains, including a drop in recent accuracy after good earlier performance); proficient (at least 10 attempts, overall accuracy of at least 80%, recent accuracy of at least 70%, consistency of at least 0.55, no repeated conceptual misunderstanding); mastered (at least 15 attempts, overall and recent accuracy of at least 85%, consistency of at least 0.75, at least 2 question formats, at least 60% on hard questions if any were attempted, and no repeated conceptual misunderstanding or sign error). The application score is reported with the evidence but is not currently required by these rules, although the configuration file defines a threshold for it.

Overall score. The software does not calculate one overall mastery score from the four indicators. The group should report them separately or define a composite with a stated rationale.
